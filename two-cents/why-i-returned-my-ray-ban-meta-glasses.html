Why I Returned My Ray-Ban Meta Glasses After One Day

The New Zealand version can't yet do the things I bought it for. What those missing features point to matters more to anyone who buys media.

Walker Zhao · Founder, Mind & Metric Media · 13 November 2026

This month I bought a pair of Ray-Ban Meta Gen 3 glasses. The next day I took them back for a refund.

The hardware was fine. The problem was what the New Zealand version leaves out. There is no display: the Meta Ray-Ban Display model, with a small screen in the lens, isn't sold here. And there is no Muse, the personal AI agent Meta announced at Connect in September, which hasn't reached New Zealand yet. What's left is a good camera, open-ear speakers and the standard voice assistant.

I bought the glasses for the agent. Without it, they are sunglasses that take photos.

What I wanted to try

The idea behind an agent on your face is simple. It sees what you see, knows what you care about, and does things for you. You look at a jacket someone is wearing and it finds where to buy it. You mention a restaurant and it books a table. Over time it learns the things you keep coming back to and brings them up before you ask.

Those were the scenarios I wanted to test. None of them run in New Zealand today. But a day with the glasses was enough to see where this is heading, and it has a lot to do with how advertising budgets are spent.

The search box gets skipped

Google is building the commerce side of the same idea. In January it launched the Universal Commerce Protocol, an open standard that lets AI agents handle a shopping journey from discovery to checkout and after-sale support. Google says it will power checkout inside AI Mode in Search and the Gemini app, and it is testing an ad format called Direct Offers that shows a retailer's discount inside AI Mode when someone signals they want to buy.

Put Meta's agent and Google's protocol side by side and the familiar path gets shorter. For twenty years, paid search has rested on one behaviour. A person decides they want something, types it into a box and clicks a result. That moment of declared intent is the most valuable inventory in advertising, and most performance budgets are built to capture it.

An agent moves that moment. If the glasses recognise the jacket, and the agent finds it, compares prices and checks out, the person never typed a query. The intent was real, but it formed and resolved inside the agent. No results page appeared for an advertiser to bid on.

From intent to interest

What an agent works from is interest: what you look at, what you linger on, what you buy, who you follow, what you mentioned last week. It infers intent from that interest, often before you would have searched for anything.

That changes the weighting in a media plan. Intent-based buying, meaning keywords, shopping queries and retargeting people who have already raised their hand, will stay useful and efficient. But fewer decisions will pass through a search box, and more will be shaped earlier by whether a brand is already among the things a person, and their agent, is interested in. Interest-based media has usually been the harder budget to defend. It is about to become the more important one.

What advertisers can do now

Treat the product feed as a storefront. Agents read structured data rather than landing pages. Product titles, attributes, stock, price and reviews are most of what an agent will ever see of your business.

Fund interest channels on purpose. Meta, YouTube and Demand Gen build the familiarity an agent will later draw on. Judge them on incremental demand, because last-click ROAS undervalues them by design.

Fix measurement before attribution gets worse. As fewer journeys include a click, click-based attribution will credit less of what is working. Incrementality tests and marketing mix models measure outcomes without needing the click.

Keep search, and test it. Branded and high-intent search will keep converting for a while. Knowing how much of it is truly incremental tells you how much is at stake as volume moves elsewhere.

A head start, if we use it

New Zealand usually gets these features later than the US. That delay is useful. It gives local advertisers time to rebuild measurement and creative for interest-based buying before the agents arrive, instead of after search volume has already started to slide.

I'll probably buy the glasses again when Muse lands here. By then I'd like our clients' accounts to be ready for it. The question this agency starts every engagement with still applies, and it gets harder to answer from a platform dashboard as agents take over more of the journey. What would happen if we didn't spend this money?

How much of your search budget is buying customers you would have had anyway?

The first conversation is diagnostic, not commercial.
